# Once upon a time an Open Source

Text of the story as it was in the first edition in galician (2026).
The comments `<!-- p. N -->` mark the page of the book were every
fragment starts; they are useful to compare with the mock-up and to translate.

<!-- p. 6 -->

Once upon a time there was a female cat named Teky.

She was a happy cat, but she didn't have a nice and warm place where she could sleep.

One especially cold night, tired of searching for a nook that didn't
exist, she found some pieces of cardboard piled up behind a shop in the town.

<!-- p. 8 -->

She cut them out carefully, folded them, and glued them, piece by piece, and built
a box.

<!-- p. 9 -->

It wasn´t perfect, but it was hers and it was nice and warm.

That night, Teky slept straight through for the first time in a long time.

<!-- p. 10 -->

The next day, a curious kitten passed by and stood staring at the
box, amazed.

“Will you make me one just like it?” he asked her.

Teky, without giving it much thought, said yes. It seemed nice to her that something
she had made for herself could also be of use to someone else.

But then the trouble started.

<!-- p. 11 -->

**Milo**, a little white and grey kitten with long whiskers and a mischievous look,
copied the box designs and started selling boxes from his cat flap
in exchange for cans of tuna.

**Nina**, a little black cat with ears always on the alert, took the same
blueprints, altered them in her own way without asking too many questions, and upon her first
leap into her new box, everything came crashing down with a sharp crack.

Teky wanted to share her discovery with everyone, regardless of whether or
not they had any little cans to offer. The fact that Milo improved it and sold it was not a
problem: that was the very reason she had shared it.

The problem was that they only acknowledged her idea when something went wrong.

If everything worked out, the idea was theirs. Teky realized that saying "yes, sure,
take it" wasn't enough. Something more was needed if she truly wanted to help
other kittens.

<!-- p. 12 -->

So Teky went to see an owl that lived in the tallest tree in the town.
His name was **Licensius**.

He had large, attentive eyes behind round glasses. When
he spoke, he did so slowly, with the solemnity of someone who had read more rules than
anyone would ever want to read in an entire lifetime.

“I need something that states how my plans can be used,” Teky
said, “so I don’t run into any trouble.”

"If there is no note, no one has permission," Licensius replied. That's it
which almost everyone understands the other way around: they think that without a note everything is valid.
And no. No note means nothing is valid.

Licensius listened to her calmly, and led her to a shelf that occupied an
entire wall of his tree, brimming with various notes, rolled up,
piled high, and sorted by colors that only he understood.

But seeing that Teky was starting from scratch, he didn't overwhelm her with all of them:
he taught her only three, the ones almost everyone used to start with.

<!-- p. 13 -->

The first one said: "Do whatever you want with the blueprints: copy them, change them, make
boxes and sell them. You just have to keep this note attached to them, so that people know
where they came from." That was the MIT.

The second one said: "Do whatever you want with the blueprints, including selling the
boxes you make using them. But if you pass on a box made this way to
someone else, you must also provide the blueprints, along with the same note, so that they can see
how it was made and remake it if they wish." That is how the GPL works.

And the third: "Do whatever you want with the blueprints, just like with the first one. And if
there’s any part or trick that would normally require special permission from
its inventor, consider this note your authorization, so that no one can
come after you for it later." That’s the Apache.

Teky read them slowly, one by one, and chose the second one.

She wanted anyone to be able to do whatever they wished with her blueprints:
modify them, build boxes using them, or sell those boxes as Milo did, without it
being an issue, provided they didn't forget their origin and that others
might also have the blueprints.

And she also wanted to ensure that, if someone altered the plans in their own way and something
subsequently went wrong, as had happened to Nina, the blame would not fall on her.

The note resolved both things at once.

She attached it clearly visible on the plans, right where it couldn't possibly be missed by
anyone looking at them.

<!-- p. 14 -->

The kittens began telling their friends, birds, mice,
and other forest animals, how cozy Teky’s box was, and little by
little, everyone in the town started wanting one.

Teky took her plans, with the license note firmly attached to the top, to the
notice board in the town square.

It was the place everyone went to look and, while they were at it, to copy ideas.
She pinned them up carefully, stood looking at them for a moment, satisfied, and thought
that was it, the job was done.

<!-- p. 15 -->

But the days went by, and no one stopped to look at them. The board was
covered in all sorts of papers, piled one on top of another, and hers got lost
among the rest, like just another drop in the town square fountain.

No one knew that there, beneath so much paper, something new was
waiting.

<!-- p. 16 -->

Feeling sad, Teky sat on the edge of the fountain, where she met his friend Tui,
a bright blue little bird who could never stay still for even a
second. Tui would fly from branch to branch, land, take flight again, always with
something to tell someone.

“If you don’t tell anyone, no one will know it exists,” Tui said to her.

<!-- p. 17 -->

And without waiting for an answer, he took flight to the highest branch of the tree in the
square. From there, with his beak wide open and his wings spread, he began to chirp
incessantly, so loudly and so rapidly that the news spread from beak to beak
throughout the entire forest in a matter of hours.

“New blueprints, fresh out of the box! Warm, comfortable box
luxury! And today, today only... for free!”

<!-- p. 18 -->

Only then did people begin to approach the square, curious to see what
that little bird was talking about so much.

Upon arrival, many would stand staring at the blueprints, not quite knowing
where to begin. How exactly was it supposed to be assembled? What was the purpose of each piece? Who
should they ask if something didn’t fit? Here, Teky learned something
else: simply hanging up the blueprints and letting people know about them wasn’t enough.

If she wanted anyone to be able to build their own box without help,
she needed to add clear instructions.

<!-- p. 19 -->

That same afternoon, she sat down to write them, in a great rush, with her
handwriting becoming increasingly cramped and her ideas tumbling over one another.

The result was... well, let's just say that even she herself wasn't entirely clear when
rereading them.

But it was a beginning, and beginnings, however imperfect they may be,
serve as a basis for improvement later on.

<!-- p. 20 -->

Little by little, other forest animals drew near, blueprints and
tangled instructions in hand, ready to assemble their own. And, without anyone
asking, some even began to lend a hand with Teky’s original
box.

**Beak**, a classic red-and-black woodpecker, always getting straight to the point,
reinforced a corner that was buckling in the wind without asking permission or offering
explanations: he simply saw the problem and fixed it.

**Ochre**, a big, fluffy-tailed squirrel with a keen eye for the cute,
painted some colored signs so that it was better to see where you entered
in the box.

**Dam**, a cautious beaver who always carried a small notebook with him
to jot things down, pointed out that the box didn't hold up well against water when
it rained hard. It was better to know this before it became a serious problem,
rather than afterwards.

<!-- p. 21 -->

**Babel**, a brightly colored and very gesticulating parrot, translated the
instructions into another language of the forest, so that those who did not understand the
first one could understand them just the same.

**Gardian**, a porcupine with its quills always on alert, and a little white
hat pulled down to its eyebrows, inspected every corner of the box, searching for
holes and cracks through which an uninvited guest might slip in, and
finished with a lock to prevent miscreants.

Each one contributed a small something from what they knew how to do, without anyone
asking them to. Teky realized that she didn’t have to know everything, nor did
everyone else have to know how to do the same things she did.

Every bit of help, however small or invisible, made the box a little
better.

<!-- p. 23 -->

**Bandit, Moon and Tinker.** One afternoon, a group of raccoons with their natural masks that made them
look like little rascals, though they weren't entirely so, came over to look at Teky's box with
great interest.

“We really like it,” said Bandit, holding something shiny between his paws,
“but we always come in through the back.”

So, without asking anyone’s permission and without causing any harm, they made
a copy of the blueprints and went off to experiment on their own, in their own
way, with what they needed. They painted their box midnight blue
with stars, added lights, dug a couple of secret tunnels,
included a trapdoor at the back, and, of course, dedicated an entire drawer just
for storing shiny things.

After a few weeks of experimenting, they saw Teky again. Not to
ask for anything, but to propose two ideas they had come up with along
the way: a magnetic latch to keep the door from blowing open in the wind, and
some small lights so they could read at night without disturbing anyone.

Teky then realized that copying the blueprints hadn't been a bad thing at all.

When copying to create something different, one can experiment without
breaking anyone else’s box, and sometimes those very ideas end up
improving the original box as well.

<!-- p. 24 -->

By summer, Teky’s box was already famous throughout the forest. There were boxes
in the trees, boxes hidden in caves, and boxes on the rooftops. Each
animal had made it a little bit their own. But there was a problem:

every time something went wrong, EVERYONE ended up looking at Teky.

<!-- p. 26 -->

She couldn't keep up with answering questions all on her own, at all hours, for
the entire forest. So, one afternoon, she hung up, next to the blueprints, which were already nearly
worn out from being copied so often, a new sign that read: "Help Wanted."

And it worked better than expected: by the next morning, three
new animals were already ready to lend a hand. The problem was that none of them
knew quite where to start, and questions from everyone —old and new alike—
kept piling up, note after note, on the very same board
where Tui had announced the box some time ago. The board, overflowing,
was starting to look more like a mess than a help.

That was when Norma the ant appeared, always with a clipboard under
her arm and a foreman’s cap that was a bit too big for her. Norma
looked at the board, looked at the little notes piled up, and decided that
the situation needed some order. She took the loose requests and organized them into
clear columns: “ready to start,” “need more hands,” and “already
underway.”

And she wrote them down on colored notes so that anyone could know at a
glance what was needed, without having to ask anyone.

Babel, Dam, Beak... and other animals that had been for some time
lending a hand they could take a marker and mark the notes that were coming,
so that others would know where to start without disturbing Teky in the
middle of her nap.

They also left a small mailbox, reserved for urgent or
delicate messages that were best not left in plain sight for the whole forest to see.

<!-- p. 28 -->

Over time, other animals kept arriving in the town, eager to set up
their own boxes, but Teky could now sleep peacefully in the sun
whenever she wished.

The first box belonged to Teky. But, as time went by, the box ended up
belonging to everyone who had put a part of themselves into it.

<!-- p. 29 -->

Teky, looking at her box now filled with the traces of others, realized that
creating an open project had never been just about leaving the blueprints out in the town square. It had meant
establishing clear ground rules, sharing the idea with the rest of the town, and allowing others
to touch it, tinker with it, and, little by little, make it their own as well.

<!-- p. 32 -->

Snip, snap, snout, this tale's told out.
